{# ================= 1. THE READ ================= #}
01 — The read

It finds the quiet hours first, because that is where a house should be predictable.

Overnight load is the part of the day least explained by people being awake. Home Energy Watch takes the median of your quiet-hour averages as a baseline, then flags each night against two rules you set: a floor in kilowatts, and a multiple of that baseline.

Every flag names the rule that fired.

No night is flagged without a reason you can read. When you disagree with a rule, change it — the night window, the floor, the multiplier, and the timezone are all yours, and the record recomputes against the new ones.

  • 01Median of quiet-hour averages becomes the baseline, or you name a specific reference day.
  • 02Floor rule flags any night whose average sits at or above your minimum kilowatts.
  • 03Multiple rule flags any night running at or above your multiple of the baseline.
  • 04Interval alerts catch the single reading that jumped, with the size of the jump and the hour's usual level.

03 — The house

Measure the meter against what your house can actually draw.

A number is only strange relative to something. List what you own and roughly what it pulls, and the tool gives you two useful ceilings to compare every reading against.

Two ceilings, one gap.

Everything on is the total if every tracked load ran at once — a reading above it means something is running that you have not listed. Mostly off is just the always-on equipment, which is what your overnight baseline should be sitting near.

{# ================= 4. THE RECORD ================= #}
04 — The record

Uploads stack up instead of overwriting each other.

Utility exports overlap. Home Energy Watch merges each new file into the history you already have and tells you exactly what it did with every reading — including the ones it refused to touch.

Nothing is quietly overwritten.

A reading is identified by its account, start time, and duration. If a new file repeats a reading you already have with the same value, it is counted as already present. If it repeats one with a different value, that is a conflict — it is counted, skipped, and left for you to look at.

  • →Compare two exports and the tool aligns them year-over-year or month-over-month, and tells you which it used.
  • →Months without a match on the other side are left out of the totals and listed separately.
  • →Each comparison writes a Markdown packet and a CSV of the aligned rows.
  • →Saved reports are access-controlled, not just hard-to-guess links.

{# ================= 6. LIMITS ================= #}
What it does not do

The honest boundaries, up front.

This tool is used in disputes with utilities, so it is worth being exact about where it stops. If any of the following is what you need, this is not it yet.

×
It does not connect to your utility for you.

There is no automatic Duke or Green Button Connect sync. You download the export yourself and upload it. Manual upload is the supported path.

×
It never signs in on your behalf.

No password scraping, no browser extension, no mobile-app capture. Your utility credentials stay yours.

×
It is not live monitoring.

There is no smart-meter stream and no device-level feed. You are reading history, at whatever resolution your utility exported.

×
It does not detect theft.

It measures load and reports which rule fired. Tampering, a failing appliance, a new tenant, and a forgotten space heater all look similar in interval data.

×
It does not fill in missing readings.

Gaps in your export stay gaps. Nothing is smoothed, interpolated, or estimated into the record.

×
Utility lookup is North Carolina only.

Matching a service address to an electric company works against NC service-territory data. Elsewhere, type the utility name yourself.
